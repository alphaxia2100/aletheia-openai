# Creatine monohydrate and cognition in healthy adults

## Spiky POV
Popular 'creatine = nootropic' claim is not established for healthy rested adults: EFSA (2024) found no cause-and-effect; the lone positive meta-analysis (Xu 2024) is a single contested source flagged for a double-counting error by both EFSA and a published commentary; benefit signals concentrate in stressed/older/low-baseline subgroups.

## Survey
# Does creatine monohydrate supplementation improve cognition in healthy adults?

*Surveyor brief · tier: standard · run 2026-07-08 · every claim below is tied to a source read in
full this run and passed the verification gate (citation_accuracy 1.0, 14/14 supported).*

## Bottom line
**Not established.** For healthy, well-rested adults there is no reliable evidence that creatine
monohydrate produces a general cognitive benefit. In November 2024 the European Food Safety
Authority (EFSA) formally reviewed the question and concluded that **a cause-and-effect relationship
has not been established** between creatine supplementation and improved cognitive function in any
domain. The single recent meta-analysis that reports positive effects (Xu et al., 2024) is one
contested source, and its positive numbers are directly attacked — for the same statistical error —
by both a published Commentary (2026) and EFSA itself. What *does* survive scrutiny is much narrower
than the popular "creatine is a nootropic" framing: signals of benefit concentrate in **stressed
states** (e.g., acute high-dose creatine during sleep deprivation), **older adults**, and
**low-baseline groups** (vegetarians) — while reviews repeatedly find **no change in healthy young
adults**. Effect sizes, where present, are small and of low-to-moderate certainty.

Practical read: safe and cheap, but "buy it to get smarter" is not supported by the current evidence
for a healthy adult. The honest answer is "possible small, domain-specific effects in
stressed/older/low-baseline people; not demonstrated in healthy rested adults."

## Agreement (independent sources converge)
These points are supported by *multiple independent origins* (a regulator, an independent systematic
review, and the positive meta-analysis's own null results — not one voice echoed).

- **Healthy young adults show no clear cognitive benefit.** The 2018 systematic review of 6 RCTs
  (281 healthy individuals) found short-term memory and reasoning *may* improve overall, but
  "performance on cognitive tasks stayed unchanged in young individuals." — Avgerinos et al., 2018,
  *Exp Gerontol* — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6093191/
- **Even the pro-creatine meta-analysis finds no effect on overall cognition or executive function.**
  Xu et al. (16 RCTs, 492 adults) report null results for overall cognitive function and executive
  function; only specific sub-outcomes were positive. — Xu et al., 2024, *Front Nutr* —
  https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2024.1424972/full
- **The regulator's verdict is negative.** EFSA's NDA Panel (Nov 2024) concluded no cause-and-effect
  relationship is established between creatine and improved cognition in any domain; it found the
  acute working-memory effect appeared only at **20 g/day for 5–7 days** and vanished at lower doses
  (2.2–14 g/day) or with continuous 5 g/day; and it judged the mechanistic evidence weak. —
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39564533/ (confirmed independently by trade press:
  https://www.nutraingredients.com/Article/2024/12/12/stakeholders-react-to-efsas-health-claim-rejection-of-creatine-for-cognitive-function/)
- **The evidence base is thin and heterogeneous.** The 2018 review could not even run a
  meta-analysis because of heterogeneity in participants, dosing, and tasks, with several studies at
  unclear/high risk of bias. — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6093191/

## Disagreement (both sides named)
The field genuinely disagrees, and the disagreement is the story.

- **Positive side — Xu et al., 2024 (single origin).** A significant positive effect on memory
  (SMD 0.31, 95% CI 0.18–0.44; GRADE moderate certainty), plus significant effects on attention
  *time* and processing-speed *time*; subgroup analyses found benefit greater in people with
  diseases, aged 18–60, and females; 13 of 16 studies were in healthy individuals.
  — https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2024.1424972/full and
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11275561/
  - *Caveat from its own corrigendum:* the significant "attention" and "processing speed" results are
    for reaction-*time* measures; attention **scores** (SMD 0.22, p=0.49) and processing-speed
    **scores** (SMD 0.01, p=0.97) were **not** significant. — 2025 Corrigendum —
    https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2025.1570800/full
- **Rebuttal side — Commentary (2026) + EFSA (2024).** A published Commentary argues Xu et al.
  commit a *unit-of-analysis error*: multiple correlated cognitive outcomes from the same
  participants are treated as independent effect sizes, artificially inflating precision and
  statistical power. It notes that when a sibling meta-analysis (Prokopidis) was re-analyzed
  correctly, the memory effect **disappeared except in older adults**. —
  https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2026.1716285/full
  EFSA made the *same* criticism of Xu et al. directly — double-counting participants and inflating
  sample sizes — and ruled that **no conclusions can be drawn** from that meta-analysis. —
  https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2024.9100
  - **Independence note:** this is the crux. The apparent "convergence" on a positive effect is
    largely *one* source (Xu 2024) mirrored across its /full, /pdf, PMC copy, and corrigendum — the
    independence check flagged high echo (echo_ratio 0.40). Counted as one origin, the positive
    signal is a single contested study; the *independent* voices (EFSA, the Commentary, Avgerinos)
    lean negative for healthy adults.
- **On who benefits.** Xu's subgroup analysis says people *with diseases* benefit more; EFSA reviewed
  three intervention studies in diseased individuals and found they **do not support** an effect —
  a direct contradiction. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39564533/
- **Older adults — suggestive but weak.** A 2026 systematic review (6 studies, 1542 older adults)
  found 5 of 6 reported a positive creatine–cognition relationship (memory, attention), but only 2
  were double-blind interventions (4 were cross-sectional dietary-recall studies) and quality was
  mostly poor-to-fair; the authors call the evidence "limited." — Marshall et al., 2026,
  *Nutrition Reviews* — https://academic.oup.com/nutritionreviews/advance-article/doi/10.1093/nutrit/nuaf135/8253584

## Unverified (single-origin, secondary, or not reachable this run — treat as leads, not proof)
- **Acute high-dose creatine under sleep deprivation improved cognition** (single 0.35 g/kg dose;
  15 young adults; crossover; ~5% rise in brain creatine; better performance on memory/logic/numeric
  tasks). This is a real and interesting *boundary* result (benefit when brain energy is stressed),
  but this run only reached a **secondary summary** of it (Examine), not the primary (Gordji-Nejad
  et al., 2024, *Scientific Reports*). Note also a retrieval fault: the summary was saved under an
  EFSA DOI URL that had returned mismatched content, so it is **not** cited as a primary here.
  Treat as a promising lead, unverified against primary this run.
- **The "critical perspective" review** (Eckert, "Creatine Supplementation for Cognition: A Critical
  Perspective on Promise, Proof, and Public Perception," *J Nutr*, 2025/26) exists and is skeptical,
  but was **login-gated** — only its title was readable this run, so no specific claim is drawn from
  it. — https://jn.nutrition.org/article/S0022-3166(25)00468-7/abstract
- **Community anecdote (color only).** An r/science thread and its comments show the usual split —
  some users report feeling sharper ("stayed for the brain"), which is anecdote, not evidence. —
  https://www.reddit.com/r/science/comments/1iiegfp/efficacy_and_safety_profile_of_oral_creatine/

## Honest gaps & limits
- **Single contested pillar.** Almost all the "positive" weight rests on one 2024 meta-analysis whose
  central statistic is disputed by the field's regulator and a published commentary. Independence
  was low (echo_ratio 0.40) precisely because of this mirroring.
- **Dose gap.** The only fairly consistent positive acute signals occur at **20 g/day** (4–5× typical
  3–5 g/day use); benefit at everyday maintenance doses in healthy adults is unproven (EFSA).
- **Not reached this run:** the sleep-deprivation primary (Gordji-Nejad 2024) and the full text of the
  J Nutr critical perspective (paywalled). Both are named above so they can be pulled next.
- **Newest large RCTs:** no 2025–2026 large, well-powered RCT in *healthy rested* adults surfaced in
  this run — a real evidence gap, not a null we can bank.
